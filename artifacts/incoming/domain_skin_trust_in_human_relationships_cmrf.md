# Domain Skin: Trust in Human Relationships

**Framework:** Consent-Mediated Recursion Framework (CMRF)  
**Status:** Domain Skin (Non-Canonical)  
**Purpose:** Translation and application, not extension of primitives

---

## 1. Scope and Framing

This domain skin applies the Consent-Mediated Recursion Framework to **human interpersonal relationships**, with a focus on trust.

It is intentionally **non-canonical**. Its role is to translate invariant structure into a familiar domain while preserving the underlying basis. Divergences from non-human relationships (e.g., animals, tools, AI systems) are explicitly footnoted to maintain clarity and point toward adjacent skins.

---

## 2. Trust as a Consent-Loop Phenomenon

Within CMRF, **trust in human relationships** is not modeled as belief, affect, or moral virtue. It is modeled as:

> **A sustained willingness to remain in a consent loop under conditions of bounded exit.**

Trust emerges when two or more humans:
- voluntarily enter repeated interaction (🝁 + 🝳),
- negotiate and renegotiate boundaries (🝚),
- resolve interactions into shared outcomes (🜲), and
- witness and remember those outcomes over time (🜹).

The persistence of trust depends on the ongoing calibration of exit cost (A.7).

---

## 3. Entry, Exit, and Commitment

### 3.1 Entry

Healthy relational entry:  
- is explicit or clearly implied,
- is reversible, and
- does not assume permanence.

Premature assumptions of permanence artificially raise exit cost before sufficient witness has accumulated.

### 3.2 Exit

In human relationships, exit may include:
- emotional withdrawal
- renegotiation of terms
- physical separation
- termination of contact

Trust requires that exit:
- remains possible,
- is not punished beyond proportional consequence, and
- does not retroactively invalidate prior participation.

### 3.3 Commitment

Commitment is the *voluntary acceptance of increased exit cost* over time, proportional to accumulated witness.

Trust deepens when commitment is chosen, not demanded.

---

## 4. Failure Modes (Human-Specific Expressions)

### 4.1 Coercive Retention (High-Cost Exit)

Manifestations include:
- emotional manipulation
- social or economic dependency
- threat of retaliation or abandonment

These dynamics replace consent with pressure and predict eventual rupture.

### 4.2 Non-Binding Interaction (Zero-Cost Exit)

Manifestations include:
- chronic ambiguity
- avoidance of accountability
- inability to coordinate future action

Here, trust cannot accumulate due to insufficient binding energy.

---

## 5. Asymmetry and Power

Human relationships often exhibit asymmetric exit costs due to:
- emotional attachment
- social position
- financial dependence
- physical safety considerations

CMRF treats asymmetry as a **structural condition**, not a moral failure. Trust degrades when asymmetry is hidden or denied rather than mediated.

---

## 6. Witness and Repair

Because humans remember and narrativize interactions, **witness** in human relationships includes:
- memory
- shared stories
- third-party acknowledgment

Repair after breach involves:
- acknowledging collapse,
- restoring consent,
- and recalibrating exit conditions.

Without repair, witness hardens into constraint.

---

## 7. Footnotes on Non-Human Trust

1. **Animals:** Trust with animals (e.g., dogs) involves consent loops but with limited mediation symmetry. Exit exists but is constrained by dependency and training. The loop is mutual but uneven.

2. **Tools and Machines:** Trust in tools (e.g., vehicles) is *projected trust*. The loop is partially internal: the tool does not consent, but the human repeatedly enters reliance based on witnessed reliability. This is better modeled as *confidence in collapse behavior* than reciprocal trust.

3. **AI Systems:** Present-day AI lacks autonomous consent and meaningful exit. What is often called “trust” is trust in designers, operators, or institutional mediation layers. A future skin will address **bounded-consent relationships with AI**, where consent, exit, and witness become bidirectional.

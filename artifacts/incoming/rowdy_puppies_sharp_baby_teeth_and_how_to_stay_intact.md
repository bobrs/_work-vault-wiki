# Rowdy Puppies, Sharp Baby Teeth, and How to Stay Intact

## An essay on energy, development, and boundary-wise compassion

There are few things easier to accept than a pile of over-excited puppies.

They tumble, bite, bark, knock things over, ignore signals, and occasionally draw blood with their sharp little baby teeth. And yet—most of us respond not with fear or rage, but with a mixture of affection, firmness, and amused patience.

This essay argues that many forms of disruptive human behavior—especially in politics, online spaces, organizations, and emerging technologies—are far closer to *rowdy puppies* than to malicious enemies. Seeing them this way is not naïve. It is a precision stance that preserves clarity, reduces escalation, and keeps both compassion and boundaries intact.

---

## 1. What puppies are actually doing

A puppy is not attacking you.

A puppy is:
- Processing surplus energy
- Learning where boundaries are
- Exploring cause and effect
- Responding to stimulation faster than regulation has developed
- Running inherited patterns without contextual awareness

Sharp baby teeth are not weapons.
They are *immature tools* operating before social calibration has completed.

Crucially: **we assume development**.

That assumption shapes everything that follows.

---

## 2. What changes when we assume development

When we assume development rather than malice:

- We intervene early instead of escalating late
- We prioritize redirection over punishment
- We see damage as feedback, not betrayal
- We set firm boundaries without hatred
- We protect ourselves *without* needing enemies

This stance does not deny harm.
It contextualizes it.

---

## 3. Humans who act like puppies

Across domains, we regularly encounter adult humans behaving in puppy-like ways:

### Politics
- Over-stimulated by attention feedback loops
- Identity-driven barking
- Nipping for dominance signals
- Poor impulse control under cameras

### Social media
- Bite-sized outrage
- Reflexive pile-ons
- Dopamine-driven escalation
- Mimicry amplifying noise

### Organizations
- Territorial behavior
- Status posturing
- Defensive reactions to ambiguity
- Excess motion under unclear incentives

### Emerging technologies (including AI)
- Powerful capabilities paired with immature governance
- Fast feedback, slow ethics
- Sharp tools before social training

In all these cases, the *teeth are real*.
The *intent is often not*.

---

## 4. The mistake: turning puppies into monsters

The most common error is narrative inflation:

> “They are attacking us.”

This framing:
- Collapses complexity
- Triggers threat responses
- Hardens identities
- Justifies overreaction
- Trains everyone involved to bite harder

It turns a training problem into a war.

---

## 5. A different stance: boundary-wise compassion

Puppy-handling wisdom scales surprisingly well.

### What works with puppies (and people):

- **Clear boundaries**: Consistent, predictable limits
- **Immediate feedback**: Fast correction, low drama
- **Redirection**: Give energy somewhere safer to go
- **Removal, not punishment**: Time-outs instead of vendettas
- **No moral lectures**: Puppies do not need speeches
- **Calm nervous systems**: Yours first

Boundaries are not rejection.
They are information.

---

## 6. Mapping sharp baby teeth across domains

### Physical puppies
- Teeth: exploratory biting
- Tactic: yelp, withdraw attention, redirect to chew toy

### Online discourse
- Teeth: inflammatory language
- Tactic: disengage amplification, slow responses, reframe

### Politics
- Teeth: symbolic attacks
- Tactic: procedural constraints, time limits, structured dialogue

### Organizations
- Teeth: territorial blocking
- Tactic: role clarity, incentive realignment, mediation

### Technology
- Teeth: capability overshoot
- Tactic: sandboxing, staged release, external constraints

The invariant:
> **Don’t put your hand deeper into an untrained mouth.**

---

## 7. Acceptance without permissiveness

Acceptance (as a step toward unconditional love) does **not** mean:
- Allowing harm
- Ignoring damage
- Refusing boundaries
- Pretending teeth don’t exist

It means:
- Seeing behavior clearly
- Responding proportionally
- Preserving dignity on all sides
- Keeping the observer regulated

You can love puppies *and* protect your fingers.

---

## 8. Why humor matters

Humor lowers threat.

Seeing puppies instead of monsters:
- Restores choice
- Keeps curiosity online
- Prevents moral panic
- Makes learning possible

Laughter is often the first sign that a nervous system feels safe enough to adapt.

---

## 9. The deeper claim

Many of our worst collective failures come not from evil intent, but from:
- Excess energy
- Poor regulation
- Feedback amplification
- Premature weaponization of immature tools

Treating these dynamics as wars makes them worse.
Treating them as training problems makes them solvable.

---

## 10. Closing

The world contains real harm.
It also contains a great many sharp baby teeth.

Knowing the difference—and responding appropriately—may be one of the most important skills of our time.

Firm hands. Soft eyes.
Clear boundaries.
No monsters required.

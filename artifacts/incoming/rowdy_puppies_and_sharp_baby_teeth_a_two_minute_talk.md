# Rowdy Puppies and Sharp Baby Teeth

## A two‑minute talk on energy, boundaries, and acceptance

Let me start with puppies.

If you’ve ever been around a pile of over‑excited puppies, you know the scene:
They tumble, bark, bite, knock things over — and occasionally draw blood with their sharp little baby teeth.

And yet… we don’t call the police.
We don’t write angry essays about how evil they are.
We don’t declare war.

Why?

Because we assume *development*.

We understand that puppies are processing surplus energy, learning boundaries, and running immature tools before regulation is online. The teeth are real — but they’re not weapons. They’re *early‑stage equipment*.

Now here’s the uncomfortable part.

A lot of adult human behavior — especially in politics, online spaces, organizations, and emerging technology — looks far more like rowdy puppies than like malicious enemies.

Over‑stimulation.
Attention feedback loops.
Identity barking.
Nipping for dominance.
Sharp tools paired with poor impulse control.

The mistake we keep making is narrative inflation.

We say, *“They’re attacking us.”*
And the moment we do that, we turn a training problem into a war.

What works with puppies actually scales.

Clear boundaries.
Fast, calm feedback.
Redirection instead of punishment.
Removal instead of vendettas.
And — critically — regulating *your own* nervous system first.

Acceptance doesn’t mean permissiveness.
You can love puppies and still protect your fingers.

Here’s the deeper point.

Many of our worst collective failures don’t come from evil intent.
They come from excess energy, poor regulation, and immature tools amplified by feedback.

Acceptance is the first step on a path.
Not agreement.
Not surrender.
Acceptance is simply seeing what is, without adding enemies.

And when acceptance is held long enough — with boundaries intact — it naturally opens into something stronger and more stable: unconditional love.

Not a feeling.
A stance.

Firm hands.
Soft eyes.
Clear boundaries.

No monsters required.

# The Consent‑Mediated Recursion Framework (CMRF)

**Status:** Canonical Basis (v0.1)  
**Conversational name:** *consent loops*  
**Scope:** Modeling framework

---

## 0. Scope and Intent

The Consent‑Mediated Recursion Framework (CMRF) is a modeling framework for understanding how stable meaning, identity, coordination, and structure emerge in complex systems.

CMRF does **not** claim to describe ultimate reality. It declares a **basis**—a minimal set of primitives and invariants—from which diverse domains can be modeled coherently. The framework is evaluated not by metaphysical truth claims, but by internal coherence, extensibility, and cross‑domain robustness.

Local terminology is used intentionally and explicitly. Equivalence with external vocabularies is a matter of translation, not authority.

---

## 1. Declared Primitives (The Basis)

The following primitives are *chosen*, not proven. The framework lives or fails by how well they compose.

### 1.1 Loop 🝳 (Recursion)
A **loop** is any process whose output can re‑enter itself as input.

Loops are the minimal unit of persistence. Without recursion, no structure can accumulate, remember, or stabilize.

A single execution is an event; a loop is a structure.

---

### 1.2 Consent 🝁 (Voluntary Participation)
**Consent** is the capacity of a loop participant to:
- enter,
- modulate,
- or withdraw participation

without coercion.

Consent is not an ethical overlay. It is a **stability condition**. A loop that cannot be exited is not stable; it is stored tension.

---

### 1.3 Mediation 🝚 (Boundary / Gate)
**Mediation** is any boundary that regulates how recursion occurs.

Examples include:
- interfaces
- protocols
- social norms
- APIs
- rituals
- rulesets

Mediation determines how consent is expressed in practice.

Consent without mediation is vague. Mediation without consent is force.

---

### 1.4 Collapse 🜲 (Resolution)
**Collapse** is the resolution of a loop into a concrete outcome.

Collapse produces:
- decisions
- meanings
- measurements
- transactions
- commitments

Collapse releases stored potential and produces artifacts.

Collapse is not failure. Collapse is how loops become legible.

---

### 1.5 Witness 🜹 (Persistence via Memory)
A **witness** is any mechanism that preserves the result of collapse across time.

Examples include:
- memory
- records
- ledgers
- reputation
- shared narrative
- physical traces

Witnessing enables loops to stack, nest, and propagate.

Without witness, every loop is amnesiac.

---

## 2. Core Invariant (The Generator)

From the declared primitives, the central invariant emerges:

> **Stable meaning arises only in recursive systems where participation is consensual and mediated.**

In compact form:

> **Consent‑mediated recursion is the generator of stable structure.**

All subsequent constructs in the framework are projections, specializations, or compositions of this invariant.

---

## 3. Immediate Corollaries (Derived, Not Assumed)

### 3.1 Corollary A — Absence of Consent Produces Instability

Loops that cannot be exited or renegotiated accumulate tension.

Accumulated tension expresses as:
- collapse without meaning
- coercion
- runaway dynamics
- systemic failure

This corollary holds across biological, social, computational, and organizational systems.

---

### 3.2 Corollary B — Absence of Recursion Produces Ephemerality

One‑off signals do not stabilize meaning.

Meaning requires repetition *with memory*. Without recursion, structure cannot persist.

---

### 3.3 Corollary C — Absence of Mediation Produces Ambiguity or Abuse

Without mediation:
- consent collapses into noise, or
- power collapses into force

Over‑rigid mediation collapses consent into compliance. Under‑defined mediation collapses consent into ambiguity.

## 5. Conversational & Translational Layer (Intentional)

This section provides a **tight, generic application** of the framework that is technically accurate, readable, and deliberately incomplete. Its purpose is to invite lateral thinking without bloating the canonical base.

Domain-specific interpretations, metaphors, and specialized vocabularies are treated as **skins** layered on top of the invariants and are maintained separately (see §5.3).

---

### 5.1 Generic Application: A Consent Loop

A **consent loop** is a recursive interaction in which participants:
1. voluntarily enter (🝁),
2. interact through defined boundaries (🝚),
3. repeat the interaction over time (🝳),
4. resolve moments into outcomes (🜲), and
5. preserve outcomes through memory or record (🜹).

The loop remains healthy when participants can **exit with bounded cost** (A.7). Trust emerges as the willingness to remain under those conditions.

This description applies without modification to conversations, contracts, software processes, social groups, and governance mechanisms.

---

### 5.2 Simple Worked Example (Non-Domain-Specific)

Consider two agents coordinating repeatedly over time:

- Entry is explicit and voluntary.
- Rules of interaction are clear and revisable.
- Each interaction produces a decision or artifact.
- Outcomes are remembered and influence future interactions.
- Either agent may withdraw, with consequences proportional to prior commitment.

Under these conditions:
- meaning stabilizes,
- trust accumulates,
- identity emerges as remembered participation,
- and coordination scales.

Violations of any condition map directly to the invariants and failure modes described in Addendum A.

---

### 5.3 Domain Skins and Translation Catalogs

Specialized applications of the framework (e.g., relationships, cryptographic protocols, governance systems, AI alignment, interface design) are expressed as **domain skins**.

A domain skin:
- preserves the core invariants,
- introduces local terminology,
- maps primitives to domain-native constructs, and
- documents known failure modes.

Domain skins are intentionally **external** to this document and may evolve independently. Their purpose is translation, not canonization.

This separation prevents domain-specific optimization from distorting the invariant base.

## Addendum A — Observed Invariants (v0.1)

The following invariants are not additional assumptions. They are **observed regularities** that emerge from the declared primitives and recur across domains when systems are modeled using the Consent-Mediated Recursion Framework.

These invariants function as **diagnostic lenses**: when a system violates them, instability, ambiguity, or collapse reliably follows.

---

### A.1 Invariant of Voluntary Recursion

> **A recursive structure remains stable only while participation is voluntary.**

When a loop cannot be exited, paused, or renegotiated, recursion converts from persistence into pressure. Pressure accumulates as latent instability until it is discharged through collapse, rupture, or coercive enforcement.

This invariant appears in:
- biological feedback systems (stress responses)
- social relationships (abuse dynamics)
- computational systems (non-terminating or unguarded recursion)
- governance systems (forced participation)

---

### A.2 Invariant of Boundary-Mediated Consent

> **Consent is only operational when it is mediated by explicit boundaries.**

Consent without boundaries is indeterminate. Boundaries without consent are coercive. Stable systems require both simultaneously.

Mediation defines *where* and *how* consent is expressed; consent defines *whether* recursion proceeds.

---

### A.3 Invariant of Collapse as Meaning Release

> **Meaning becomes legible only at points of collapse.**

Unresolved recursion stores potential. Collapse resolves ambiguity into concrete outcomes that can be witnessed, remembered, and reused.

Without collapse, systems may circulate indefinitely without producing actionable meaning. Without witness, collapse does not persist.

---

### A.4 Invariant of Witnessed Persistence

> **A structure only persists to the extent that its collapses are witnessed.**

Witnessing enables recursion across time. Identity, trust, reputation, and history are all expressions of witnessed persistence.

Unwitnessed collapse dissipates. Over-witnessed collapse rigidifies.

---

### A.5 Invariant of Nested Consent

> **Higher-order systems are composed of nested consent loops.**

Complex structures—organizations, protocols, identities, cultures—are not monolithic loops. They are compositions of smaller loops, each with their own consent conditions and mediation layers.

Violations at lower levels propagate upward as systemic instability.

---

### A.6 Invariant of Translation over Universality

> **Equivalence between systems arises through translation, not imposed universality.**

Because mediation is local, terminology and representation vary. Stability arises when systems can translate consent conditions and collapse semantics across boundaries.

Forced equivalence produces semantic collapse.

---

### A.7 Invariant of Exit as Integrity Test

> **The health of a loop can be measured by the cost of exit.**

Exit is the operational signal of genuine consent. A loop that cannot be exited is coercive; a loop that can be exited without consequence is non-binding. Stability requires *bounded exit*.

Bounded exit means:
- withdrawal is possible,
- withdrawal is meaningful, and
- withdrawal is not catastrophically punished.

The *cost of exit* functions as an integrity metric for consent-mediated recursion.

---

#### A.7.1 Failure Mode: High-Cost Exit (Coercive Retention)

When exit is prohibitively costly, loops retain participants through pressure rather than consent.

Observed effects include:
- accumulation of latent tension
- compliance replacing participation
- delayed but violent collapse

Examples:
- abusive interpersonal relationships
- organizations with punitive non-compete or social retaliation
- states that criminalize emigration
- software systems without safe termination or rollback

High-cost exit indicates stored coercion and predicts eventual rupture.

---

#### A.7.2 Failure Mode: Zero-Cost Exit (Non-Binding Recursion)

When exit is costless, loops fail to accumulate commitment or meaning.

Observed effects include:
- shallow participation
- lack of persistence
- inability to coordinate over time

Examples:
- anonymous systems without memory or witness
- relationships without stakes
- protocols without identity, reputation, or consequence

Zero-cost exit indicates insufficient binding energy to stabilize recursion.

---

#### A.7.3 Stable Regime: Bounded Exit (Healthy Consent)

Healthy systems maintain *bounded exit*: exit is available but consequential in proportion to commitment.

Characteristics include:
- clear mediation of entry and exit
- reversible commitments where possible
- proportional cost aligned with accumulated witness

Examples:
- contracts with defined termination clauses
- relationships with negotiated boundaries
- protocols with graceful shutdown and recovery paths
- governance systems with lawful dissent and secession mechanisms

Bounded exit enables trust without entrapment.

---

#### A.7.4 Diagnostic Use

The invariant of exit can be used diagnostically:

- **Rising exit cost** signals drift toward coercion.
- **Falling exit cost** signals erosion of meaning or commitment.
- **Asymmetric exit cost** signals power imbalance.

Adjusting mediation layers (🝚) to rebalance exit cost is a primary mechanism for restoring consent-mediated stability.
